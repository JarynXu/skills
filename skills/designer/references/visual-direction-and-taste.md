# Visual direction and taste

Use this reference when a design needs a new visual direction, feels generic despite being usable, or must deliberately evolve an existing visual language. The goal is not novelty for its own sake. Establish a coherent visual thesis that belongs to the product, then make individual choices reinforce it.

## Form a visual thesis before styling

Before choosing components, decoration, or effects, derive a short visual thesis from observable evidence:

- product purpose and emotional register;
- audience, frequency of use, and consequences of error;
- content type and density;
- brand or existing interface-system evidence;
- platform and implementation constraints;
- references the user explicitly wants to preserve, approach, or avoid.

Express the thesis as a small set of directional qualities with consequences, for example: `quiet, dense, precise` or `editorial, warm, image-led`. Avoid empty adjectives such as `modern`, `clean`, or `premium` unless they are translated into concrete decisions.

A useful thesis constrains later choices. If two proposed directions would produce essentially the same typography, composition, density, surfaces, and motion, they are not meaningfully different directions.

## Design from the product's world

Look for visual material in the product itself before reaching for familiar UI formulas:

- domain objects, instruments, documents, environments, or workflows;
- characteristic geometry, scale, texture, imagery, language, or data;
- cultural or platform conventions users already understand;
- moments where the product should feel restrained, expressive, trustworthy, fast, tactile, or ceremonial.

Translate those signals rather than decorating the interface with literal motifs. A finance product does not need coins; a developer tool does not need terminal-green text. The relationship may instead appear in density, precision, typography, motion, or information treatment.

## Make hierarchy carry the design

Prefer hierarchy, proportion, rhythm, typography, and composition over ornamental effects. Establish:

1. what the eye should encounter first;
2. what supports that decision;
3. what can recede until requested;
4. where the composition needs tension, pause, or contrast;
5. which repeated relationships create rhythm across the surface.

Do not give every region its own card, heading, border, background, and icon. Containers are useful when they express grouping, interaction, elevation, or state; they are harmful when they merely compensate for weak hierarchy.

## Take bounded aesthetic risks

When the brief permits exploration, make one or two consequential choices rather than many decorative ones. Examples include a distinctive type scale, asymmetric composition, unusual but legible density, a strong image treatment, a restrained material system, or a characteristic motion behavior.

A risk is justified when it strengthens product identity or task comprehension and survives realistic content and accessibility pressure. Remove it when it exists only to make the screenshot look designed.

## Avoid default-agent aesthetics

Treat these as warning signs, not universal prohibitions:

- a centered hero followed by interchangeable feature cards regardless of product structure;
- decorative gradients used as a substitute for a color system;
- excessive rounded containers and floating panels without semantic reason;
- equal visual emphasis across every section;
- generic sans-serif typography with no deliberate hierarchy or voice;
- repeated badges, pills, glows, and icons that add visual activity but little meaning;
- empty dashboard chrome created before understanding the data and decisions;
- copying the surface appearance of a reference while ignoring its underlying spacing, typography, hierarchy, and interaction logic.

When a familiar pattern is the correct answer, use it deliberately. Familiarity is not a defect; unexamined defaulting is.

## Explore by meaningful axes

When multiple directions are useful, vary named axes such as:

- composition: centered / asymmetric / split / immersive;
- density: spacious / balanced / compact;
- voice: neutral / editorial / technical / playful;
- surface model: flat / layered / material / image-led;
- typography: restrained utility / expressive display / document-like;
- motion: nearly instant / physically responsive / narrative.

Keep product requirements and core content stable so the comparison reveals the effect of the visual direction rather than a different product concept.

## Judge the result

A strong direction should answer yes to most of these questions:

- Does it look specific to this product rather than merely competent?
- Can the main hierarchy be understood without decorative effects?
- Do typography, color, spacing, surfaces, imagery, and motion feel like one language?
- Are the distinctive choices repeated as a system rather than isolated flourishes?
- Does realistic content strengthen rather than break the composition?
- Can the direction extend to neighboring surfaces without cloning one layout?
- Is anything visually loud without earning that emphasis?

If the design is usable but generic, do not add random polish. Revisit the visual thesis and the few high-leverage decisions that express it.
