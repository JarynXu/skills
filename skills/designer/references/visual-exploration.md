# Visual exploration

Use this reference when the user asks for alternatives, the product has no established visual language, the current direction is demonstrably generic or weak, or a consequential visual decision benefits from comparison before commitment. Do not manufacture variants for routine continuation work with an approved design language.

## Explore decisions, not screenshots

Define the design question before producing alternatives. Keep the product behavior, required content, and acceptance boundaries stable unless those are themselves under exploration.

Choose one or more named axes that can materially change the answer, such as:

- composition and information staging;
- density and rhythm;
- typographic voice;
- surface/depth model;
- imagery or illustration role;
- interaction model;
- motion character.

A variant must occupy a meaningfully different position on at least one important axis. Color-only, radius-only, or copy-only changes do not count as distinct visual directions unless that variable is genuinely the decision under test.

## Limit the set

Default to three directions when the design space is open. Use two when the decision is narrow and up to five only when the user requests broad exploration or the axes genuinely support it.

Name directions by their design logic rather than Option A/B/C. For each direction, be able to state:

- its dominant thesis;
- which axes differ;
- why it could fit the product;
- what it gives up.

If two directions converge during design, remove or replace one rather than padding the set.

## Keep comparison fair

Use the same realistic content, core task, viewport, and required states across variants. Preserve existing brand assets and system constraints unless their replacement is explicitly part of the exploration.

Do not make one direction intentionally weaker. Every candidate should be plausible to ship if its tradeoff is chosen.

When implementation or design-tool access permits, present variants at useful scale in realistic context. Avoid tiny side-by-side thumbnails as the only evidence for spacing, typography, or interaction quality.

## Test beyond the beauty state

For each serious direction, inspect at least one condition that stresses it:

- long or localized content;
- dense data;
- validation or error state;
- narrow viewport;
- empty/loading state;
- repeated daily use;
- reduced motion or high contrast where relevant.

A direction that works only for a hero frame is not a product visual language.

## Compare through consequences

Evaluate each candidate against the same questions:

- Does hierarchy support the primary task?
- Does the direction belong to this product and audience?
- What becomes easier to scan, understand, trust, or remember?
- What cost does the direction introduce in density, flexibility, accessibility, implementation, or maintenance?
- Can its principles extend to neighboring surfaces?
- Which distinctive choices are systematic rather than decorative?

Do not choose a winner solely from novelty. The strongest direction is the one whose tradeoffs best fit the product.

## Converge deliberately

When the user or design authority chooses a direction, preserve the reasons for the choice and promote the underlying principles, not a frozen screenshot.

Integrate the selected direction into applicable foundations, components, and surfaces within scope. Do not casually blend rejected directions back into the result unless a specific trait solves an identified problem without weakening coherence.

If no candidate is adequate, state what each taught and run another round around the most promising axis rather than polishing all of them indefinitely.
