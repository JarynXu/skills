# Imagery and photography art direction

Use this reference when photography, generated imagery, illustration, product rendering, portraits, or image-led visual assets materially determine the design. The goal is to direct images as part of the product's visual system rather than treat them as interchangeable decoration.

## Give the image a job

Before choosing a style, decide what the image must do:

- prove or demonstrate a product, place, person, or result;
- establish identity or emotional tone;
- explain structure, material, scale, or use;
- create a visual anchor for a campaign or hero;
- provide context around an interface or story;
- support comparison or a sequence;
- decorate a low-risk surface without competing with the task.

The image's job determines its required subject clarity, detail, realism, crop tolerance, and relationship to text. Do not use a dramatic image when the design needs neutral evidence, or a generic stock image when identity is the point.

## Separate subject, support, and environment

Define:

1. **Hero subject** — what must remain unmistakable.
2. **Supporting elements** — props, people, labels, or context that explain the hero.
3. **Environment** — the space, backdrop, atmosphere, or setting that locates the image.

Supporting elements should strengthen recognition, scale, use, or narrative. Remove random props that merely fill empty regions or add “premium” texture.

Control subject/background separation through scale, focus, light, contrast, color, depth, or negative space rather than outlining everything artificially.

## Direct viewpoint before effects

The camera or implied viewpoint changes meaning before any color grade does.

Specify as needed:

- camera height and angle: eye-level, overhead, low, oblique, axial, isometric;
- distance: environmental, full-body, medium, close, macro/detail;
- lens character: wide/contextual, natural/neutral, compressed/isolating;
- depth of field and which plane must stay sharp;
- crop and how edges interact with the subject;
- perspective accuracy when geometry, products, architecture, or diagrams matter.

Use these terms to describe the desired spatial relationship, not as ornamental technical jargon. An exact camera model or lens number is useful only when it changes the intended look or downstream production.

## Build a coherent light model

Treat lighting as a spatial system:

- identify the key source and its direction;
- choose hard or soft character according to material and mood;
- decide whether fill preserves detail or allows strong shadow;
- use rim, practical, reflected, or ambient light only when it serves separation or environment;
- keep color temperature relationships intentional;
- preserve a consistent light direction across a series when continuity matters.

Strong light is not automatically high quality. Product, documentary, editorial, and atmospheric images need different contrast. Avoid adding rim light, glow, neon, and volumetric haze simultaneously unless the scene genuinely calls for them.

## Describe materials by how they behave

A material becomes convincing when its response to light and contact is coherent.

Consider properties such as:

- matte versus glossy;
- rough versus polished;
- transparent, translucent, or opaque;
- hard, soft, fibrous, porous, wet, powdery, metallic, ceramic, glass, paper, skin, fabric, wood, or stone;
- edge behavior, thickness, seams, grain, pores, wrinkles, condensation, wear, or other scale cues.

Choose the few properties that make the subject recognizable. Do not stack unrelated material adjectives as a substitute for observation.

## Use believable imperfection deliberately

Photographic realism often needs controlled irregularity:

- natural skin texture rather than plastic smoothing;
- small asymmetries in fabric, hair, reflections, or objects;
- plausible motion blur or focus falloff;
- real contact shadows and pressure marks;
- subtle grain, noise, atmospheric variation, or surface wear when appropriate.

Imperfection should explain the medium or environment, not become a generic “film” overlay. Commercial beauty or pristine product photography may intentionally suppress some irregularity; make that a deliberate branch.

## Direct product imagery around recognition and desire

For a product-led image, establish:

- the hero product and recognizable silhouette;
- material and finish;
- viewing angle and scale;
- one coherent lighting idea;
- supporting props only when they explain use, ingredient, benefit, audience, or world;
- the brand palette and visual signature;
- where copy, labels, or badges may appear without competing with the product.

Do not let props, environmental spectacle, or decorative text become more memorable than the product unless the campaign concept deliberately calls for that tradeoff.

For a series, keep product proportions, logo treatment, light family, palette, and rendering quality stable while varying scene or message.

## Preserve identity and invariants in reference-based work

When using an existing image, person, product, or design as a reference, separate three classes before changing it:

- **Preserve** — identity, pose, orientation, product geometry, key colors, crop, environment, or other anchors that must remain.
- **Transform** — the specific attributes allowed to change, such as medium, clothing treatment, material, atmosphere, or background.
- **Do not introduce** — new people, logos, text, props, features, or structural changes that would violate the task.

State the preservation rules before the transformation. The more important the reference identity, the fewer unconstrained changes should compete with it.

## Art-direct illustration beyond a style name

A named style is insufficient. Define the composition and making logic that produce it:

- subject and gesture;
- shape language and level of simplification;
- line behavior or absence of line;
- color relationships;
- texture or physical medium;
- depth model: flat, layered, volumetric, painterly;
- edge quality and rendering finish;
- what must stay consistent across characters, scenes, or assets.

“Watercolor,” “3D,” “anime,” or “editorial illustration” should constrain technique and form, not replace the design brief.

## Make images coexist with typography and UI

Reserve a region for copy when copy is required; do not hope a usable text area appears after generation or selection.

- protect faces, products, or critical detail from overlays;
- ensure image contrast supports foreground text without heavy-handed dark gradients when a better crop or image is available;
- keep exact brand/product text out of generated imagery when it can be rendered more reliably as real interface or layout text;
- define alternate crops or focal positions for responsive layouts;
- provide meaningful alternative text or non-image access when the image carries information.

If readable text is intrinsic to the image itself, constrain the amount and verify the actual result rather than trusting the prompt or mockup.

## Keep a visual series continuous

Across related imagery, decide which variables are fixed:

- identity and product geometry;
- camera family and perspective;
- lighting direction and contrast;
- palette and color grade;
- material rendering;
- background/environment logic;
- crop and subject scale;
- illustration or retouching treatment.

Continuity is usually more valuable than making every image independently spectacular.

## Diagnose image failures at the causal layer

When imagery feels wrong, classify the problem before changing style words:

- subject unclear -> hierarchy, crop, or scale;
- fake -> light, contact, perspective, anatomy, or material response;
- generic -> subject/world specificity or art direction;
- cluttered -> supporting props or background competition;
- disconnected from brand -> palette, treatment, subject choice, or recurring signature;
- hard to use in layout -> crop, negative space, focal placement, or aspect-ratio planning;
- overproduced -> remove effects and restore one coherent camera/light/material idea.

Judge the rendered asset, not the sophistication of its production description.
